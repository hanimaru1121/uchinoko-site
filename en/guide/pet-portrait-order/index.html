Custom Pet Portraits: What to Know Before You Commission One

Published October 16, 2026

A portrait of your pet, for your profile picture, your wall, or a gift for family. Custom pet portraits vary a lot in where you get them and what they cost. This guide covers what to know before you order, so you end up with something you love.

Choose a style

  • Realistic. Fur texture, the light in their eyes. The style where likeness shows most clearly.
  • Watercolor or oil painting look. Soft and warm, great for framing.
  • Illustrated or cartoon. Rounded and cute, ideal for avatars and stickers.
  • Pixel art. Tiny squares, perfect for icons and widgets (how to make pet pixel art).

Also decide whether you want a physical piece (paper or canvas, shipped to you) or a digital file. A digital file can be printed later or used as an avatar.

How pricing works

Prices for pet portraits vary widely by style, size, number of pets, background and format (digital file or physical piece). Compare several listings and check what the base price includes. Hand-painted originals on paper or canvas, or work from well-known artists, tend to cost more. Extras like a full body, a background, a second pet, or a print-ready file usually add to the base price.

Decide before you order

  • What it's for. Avatar, framed print, merch. The use affects the size you need and the rights you should ask about.
  • Print size, if printing. A resolution of about 300 ppi (pixels per inch) is a common guideline. An 8×10 inch print needs about 2400×3000 pixels.
  • Which photos to send. A clear face shot plus a full-body shot helps the artist get the shape right (choosing photos, taking better pet photos).
  • Describe their features in words. "Her right ear folds a little." "White tip on his tail." The harder it is to see in a photo, the more it's worth mentioning.

Check before you pay

  • How many revisions, and whether you'll see a sketch before the final.
  • Delivery time. If it's a gift, leave a buffer.
  • Whether AI is used, and whether the listing says so. If you want hand-drawn, ask up front.
  • Usage rights. A profile picture or framed print at home is usually fine; selling merch often costs extra or needs separate permission.
  • Cancellation and refund terms.

The artist usually keeps the copyright (in the US)

In the US, paying for a commission doesn't by itself transfer the copyright to you. A standalone portrait usually isn't a "work made for hire," and a copyright transfer has to be in writing and signed (U.S. Copyright Office, Circular 30). For personal enjoyment this rarely matters, but if you plan to print and sell, hand out, or advertise with it, agree in writing on what you can do before you order.
